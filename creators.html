0Before you start

Ting is an iPhone app that teaches Mandarin through a feed of short, real Chinese clips. Each clip uses words the learner mostly knows, with one new word at a time. Tap any word for its pinyin and meaning.

Who every video is for: the rusty middle learner

  • Learned Chinese before (school, uni, a class, an app), then stopped.
  • Remembers lots of words, but can't follow people talking at normal speed.
  • Dramas are too hard. Beginner videos are too easy. Nothing sits in the middle.
  • Scrolls more than they'd like, and wants about 15 minutes a day that feels like progress.
I know the words, I just can't hear them when people talk. I used to be decent at Chinese.

The one rule: show the click

People download when they see the app working on a real phone. Every video shows this moment by second 15:

  • A real clip plays in Ting, at normal speed.
  • You tap a word and the pinyin and meaning pop up.
  • It clicks: suddenly you understand what they said.

Screen-record on your iPhone (Control Centre, then Screen Recording) with your own Ting account.

2Find an idea

Don't invent from scratch. Find a video that has already gone viral, keep its structure, and change the topic to Chinese and Ting. Then you know the idea works before you film it.

Entertainment

It's funny or fun to watch

Pingo and Tenmin creators get this from a learner's mistake and the app's reaction.

Education

They learn something

A word, a phrase, or the moment a fast line suddenly makes sense.

Emotion

They feel it

Frustration at Chinese, relief when it clicks, "that's me".

Where to look

Scroll like your viewer

Spend 10 minutes scrolling Instagram and TikTok the way a rusty learner would. Every time an app ad or a creator's app video stops you, save it and write down why: what question the first 3 seconds made you ask, and which E it gave you.

Look at small accounts

The best formats to copy come from accounts with few followers that still got huge views. Then it was the format that did the work, not the creator's fame.

How to tell a format is good

Small things that make a big difference

Don't say the app's name

Show Ting working, but don't say or write its name in the video. People comment "what app is this?", the comments push the video to more people, and you reply "link in my bio".

Comments about the app beat views

A video with fewer views and lots of "what app is this?" comments is worth more than a viral one nobody asks about. Those comments are the sign it sells.

3Make it: hook, buildup, payoff

Watch time decides whether a video spreads. The hook makes the viewer ask a question, the buildup makes them want the answer more, and the payoff answers it.

0 to 3 s

Hook

  • Make them ask a question.
  • Use a visual, spoken, text and sound hook together.
  • Copy hooks that already went viral.
Middle

Buildup

  • Make the question stronger.
  • Don't drag it out.
  • Hook them again every few seconds.
End

Payoff

  • Answer the question.
  • Educate, entertain or move them.
  • Put it at the very end.

Chain them: each payoff can open the next question ("will the next clip be harder?"). When there are no questions left, people stop watching.

Where this comes from: Robert McKeaveney on every great hook creating a question (1.7M views), chaining hooks and payoffs (334K views) and what to fix in each part (903K views).

4Check it before you post

Tick every box. If one fails, fix it before posting.

5Post

Once you have 1,000 followers on Instagram: make 3 to 5 versions of each idea, post them as Trial Reels (shown only to non-followers), and share the best one with everyone. Until then, post one video a day and test hooks across days.

Don't wait for perfect. Messy hair or a shaky camera doesn't matter. The format, hook, buildup and payoff do. Robert McKeaveney explains why here (402K views).

6Review the analytics (3 days after posting)

Open the video's insights and look at the retention graph: the share of viewers still watching at each second. Its shape tells you which part broke.

Steep drop at the startThe hook failed. Most people left in the first 2 or 3 seconds. Make the first second ask a clearer question.
Slow slide through the middleThe buildup failed. People got bored waiting. Cut the slow parts and re-hook every few seconds.
Drop just before the endThe payoff failed. It came too early or didn't answer the hook. Move it to the very end.

What good looks like

  • Most people are still watching after 3 seconds, then the line stays fairly flat.
  • The video beats your usual views (Instagram's dotted "typical" line). Three below it in a row lowers your next video's starting audience. Three above raises it.

What each video did

  • Views: it found new people.
  • Follows: they wanted more of you.
  • Downloads from your link and "what app is this?" comments: it sold Ting. These matter most.

Half your videos can be for reach and half for sales. One video rarely does both.

Where this comes from: Robert McKeaveney on reading your retention graph (903K views), your baseline (112K views) and the content funnel (4.3M views).

7What to do next

It did well

  • Make 3 to 5 more versions now: same idea, new hook, new clip or a new setting.
  • A month later, change 1 or 2 parts and post it again.
  • Tell Noah, so other creators can try the format too.

It didn't

  • Find the broken part in the graph (step 6) and fix only that part.
  • Keep what worked: if the hook held people, reuse it with a better payoff.
  • Post fewer videos you're confident in rather than many you're not.

Where this comes from: Robert McKeaveney on making another version of a winner (886K views; his first version got 14M views, the second 9M) and reposting old winners (175K views).
